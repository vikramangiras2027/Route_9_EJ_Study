Environmental justice (EJ) research asks whether the burdens of pollution fall disproportionately on low-income communities and communities of color. This paper examines that question along the Route 9 corridor in Worcester and Shrewsbury, Massachusetts, by combining 2024 American Community Survey (ACS) income data, the 2020 MassGIS EJ polygon layer, MassDOT traffic counts, and 2025 EPA fine particulate matter (PM2.5) monitoring data for 166 Census block groups.

Median household income rises with distance from Route 9 (Spearman ρ = +0.47, *p* < 0.001; tract-cluster bootstrap 95% CI [+0.20, +0.66]), by roughly $10,651 per kilometer. The relationship holds within Worcester alone (ρ = +0.57), so it is not simply the wealth gap between the two towns. Ninety-six percent of block groups within 1.5 km of the road are EJ-designated, compared with 84% elsewhere in the two towns (odds ratio 5.1, Fisher exact *p* = 0.009). Median tract income in the corridor is $52,011, almost exactly half of the statewide median of $103,960, versus $84,407 elsewhere in the two towns. A cumulative burden index combining modelled traffic exposure, low income, and EJ criteria identifies 33 corridor hotspot block groups housing about 42,805 EJ residents.

Air monitoring data show a 2025 mean PM2.5 of 5.65 µg/m³, below the federal annual standard of 9.0 but above the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline of 5.0, with all WHO 24-hour exceedances occurring in June through August and moving in lockstep across monitors 23 km apart. The PM2.5 data therefore describe regional air quality and cannot be attributed to Route 9. The findings document a clear spatial overlap between traffic exposure and socioeconomic disadvantage, while the data limitations mean the results are associations, not measurements of individual exposure or health outcomes.
