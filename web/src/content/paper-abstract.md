Environmental justice (EJ) research asks whether the burdens of infrastructure fall disproportionately on low-income communities and communities of color. This study examines that question along the Route 9 corridor in Worcester and Shrewsbury, Massachusetts, treating Worcester as the primary case and Shrewsbury as a sensitivity check, for 166 Census block groups.

Traffic is measured, not assumed: MassDOT segment counts (mostly 2024–2025) cover the full 17.7 km corridor, averaging 20,646 vehicles/day in Worcester and 40,615 in Shrewsbury (range 13,461–45,937; 44% of the corridor carries 30,000+). The previous single-station figure (28,112 at station M3002W) misstated both ends of the road. Block-group traffic exposure remains a distance-decay model built on this measured AADT, not a measurement at each address.

In Worcester, tract median household income rises with distance from Route 9 (Spearman ρ = +0.48, tract-cluster bootstrap 95% CI [+0.17, +0.72], n = 44 tracts); with town fixed effects the pooled slope is +$11,243 per kilometer (CI +$6,609 to +$15,876). Household-weighted pooled median income is $56,458 inside the Worcester corridor versus $82,432 in the rest of the city, and 46% of corridor households earn under $50,000 versus 31% elsewhere — about half the statewide median of $103,960. Ninety-seven percent of Worcester corridor block groups are EJ-designated versus 88% elsewhere in the city.

A traffic-independent vulnerability index (income and EJ criteria only) identifies 27 Worcester corridor block groups in the top quintile, housing about 34,339 EJ-designated residents — the study’s primary priority list for further review. A legacy cumulative burden index that folds in modelled traffic exposure flags a similar but not identical set of 33 block groups (42,416 EJ residents); because its traffic term is itself a function of distance, its correlation with distance (ρ = −0.75) is partly by construction, versus −0.45 for the traffic-independent score.

Air monitoring data show a 2025 mean PM2.5 of 5.65 µg/m³, below the federal annual standard of 9.0 but above the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline of 5.0, with all WHO 24-hour exceedances occurring in June through August and moving in lockstep across monitors 23 km apart. The nearest monitor (Worcester Summer Street) sits 0.9 km from Route 9 — an urban-background site, not a near-road one — so these data describe regional air quality and cannot be attributed to Route 9. The findings document a clear spatial overlap between measured traffic loading and socioeconomic disadvantage in Worcester, while the data limitations mean the results are neighborhood-level associations, not measurements of individual exposure, health outcomes, or causation.
